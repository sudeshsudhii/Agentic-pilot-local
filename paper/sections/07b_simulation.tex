\subsection{Baselines and Verification (Studies A and B; H1, H2)}

\input{tables/sim/baseline}
\input{tables/sim/tests}

Table~\ref{tab:sim-baseline} compares four completion rules on the same 100 held-out scenarios at two levels that must not be mixed. At the \emph{decision level}, the planner claims completion in 71 scenarios, 43 of them truly complete at that moment; the four verifiers face the same 71 claims and the same truth, so their decisions are paired. At the \emph{policy level}, an episode's final state may differ between configurations for the same scenario, because a rejected claim returns control to planning and the episode may then finish (or, after a false rejection, never be reported); task success and episode-level false completions are paired outcomes of different policies, not of one ground truth.

\emph{H1 (false completions).} Episodes end in a false completion 19 times with $\Phi$ against 28 without verification (discordant pairs 9 and 0; exact McNemar $p=0.004$ conditional on the simulation assumptions; Table~\ref{tab:sim-tests}); basic success detection removes 4. Across 1{,}000 regenerated scenario sets the paired reduction by $\Phi$ averages 0.11 false completions per scenario (2.5--97.5th percentiles 0.05--0.17), and the paired test gives $p<0.05$ in 96\% of the sets. Under the stated assumptions the simulation is therefore consistent with H1; it is not empirical confirmation.

\emph{H2 (decision reliability).} On the 71 claims, $\Phi$ decides correctly in 45 cases (accuracy 0.63, Wilson 0.52--0.74) and accepting every claim in 43 (0.61, 0.49--0.71): $\Phi$ is correct alone in 9 claims, all true rejections of premature claims, and wrong alone in 7, all false rejections (exact McNemar $p=0.80$, conditional on the assumptions). The simulation thus does not support H2 for $\Phi$ alone. The proposed enhanced validation reaches 54 of 71 (0.76), a difference that follows from its assumed detection rates. At the policy level, $\Phi$ completes 37 tasks against 43 without verification (discordant pairs 1 and 7, $p=0.07$): the false rejections cost successes that continuation after rejection only partly recovers.

\subsection{Ablation (Study C)}

\input{tables/sim/ablation}

Table~\ref{tab:sim-ablation} removes one component at a time. Removing evidence validation reproduces the no-verification row. Removing all stored evidence (a simulated configuration; the implemented switch removes only the per-action execution records) changes no decision, because the implemented $\Phi$ reads the live page rather than the stored evidence; it removes the audit trail (evidence available in 0\% instead of 89\% of episodes). Removing recovery lowers TSR from 0.37 to 0.30 and, across replications, from 0.44 to 0.38. Removing memory changes two outcomes in this set and nothing on average.

\subsection{Memory and Retrieval (Study D; H3)}

\input{tables/sim/memory}

On the synthetic memory corpus the real store returned a relevant record in the top three for each of the 9 queries that had one (recall@3 1.00, MRR 0.93 over those 9). It has no relevance threshold, however: a query with no relevant memory still returned three records, and for 3 of 10 queries the top record was irrelevant or a \emph{failed} earlier attempt (Table~\ref{tab:sim-memory}). With that measured misleading rate, the simulated repeated workflows gain nothing from memory (18 against 19 of 44 successful), so H3 is not supported. A relevance filter that drops failed attempts and unrelated records (proposed) raises contextual accuracy from 0.64 to 0.86 in the simulation.

\subsection{Recovery (Study E; H4)}

\input{tables/sim/recovery}

The real recovery engine classifies a navigation error containing \texttt{net::ERR\_} as transient, treats an invalid submission as a verification failure and assigns it re-planning, and does not recognize a missing confirmation, which it classifies as unknown (\textsc{actual}). In the simulation (Table~\ref{tab:sim-recovery}), retry-based recovery recovers 69 of 93 recoverable failures and almost none of the missing confirmations (4 of 20), and 9 undetected missing confirmations end as false completions. The proposed evidence-guided recovery, which checks an explicit confirmation postcondition, recovers 87 and leaves 2 false completions---a consequence of its assumed success rates, not evidence for it--- but it spends as many attempts on permanent failures (162).

\subsection{Verification Overhead (Study F; H5)}

\input{tables/sim/overhead}

$\Phi$ takes a median of 5.2\,ms on an ordinary page of the self-hosted sites and 26.8\,ms on the constructed gate states; a screenshot 61\,ms and DOM extraction 76\,ms (\textsc{actual}, Table~\ref{tab:sim-overhead}). In the simulation, with an assumed median of 3\,s per model call on a CPU, inference takes 84\% of an episode; on average $\Phi$ adds 0.035\,s per episode (0.2\%), evidence screenshots 0.46\,s, and a model self-check 4.5\,s (+24\% in mean episode time; +28\% in the medians of Table~\ref{tab:sim-overhead}). At 1.5\,s or 6\,s per call the median episode takes 8.9\,s or 32.6\,s. H5 is consistent with the simulation: rule-based verification is cheap, and its cost is in false rejections, not time.

The advantage of verification depends on how often the planner claims completion prematurely, which is unmeasured (Fig.~\ref{fig:sim-sens}): at $\pi=0.3$ the FCR of $\Phi$ is 0.14 against 0.21 without verification, at $\pi=0.95$ 0.32 against 0.45, while FRR is constant at about 0.16 against 0.08.
